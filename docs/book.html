Part I

Understanding AI

Three chapters to replace fog with a picture: what we are building, how machines learn from examples, and what a “language model” actually is. Everything later in the book stands on these three ideas.

Chapter 1

What Are We Actually Building?

Before any building starts, we need precise words for the thing itself — because “AI” is used to mean at least four different things.

Fontaine AI in one sentence

Fontaine AI is a program designed to learn patterns from large amounts of text, so that after training it can read text and predict — one small piece at a time — what should come next. That may sound almost insultingly simple next to headlines about “artificial intelligence,” but this simple trick, practiced by a machine at enormous scale, is what makes modern AI assistants write, answer, translate, and reason. Fontaine is your own, understandable version of that trick.

AI model vs. AI application

These two get confused constantly, and the difference matters for everything you will build:

An AI model

A mathematical object: a huge collection of adjustable numbers organized in a specific structure, which transforms input (text) into output (a prediction). By itself it has no interface, no buttons, no chat window — it is an engine.

Example: the trained Fontaine model file sitting in a checkpoint folder.

An AI application

The product people interact with: chat windows, memory of your conversation, accounts, safety filters, mobile apps. The application uses a model the way a car uses an engine.

Example: ChatGPT — the app — versus the GPT model behind it.

Fontaine AI is deliberately both, in layers. The model is the first thing we build. Around it we construct the platform: the data pipeline, tokenizer, trainer, checkpoint system, evaluator, and inference engine — the machinery a model needs to exist and be useful. An application (a chat website on top of Fontaine) would be the last layer, and the easiest one, once the first two exist.

What is a “foundation model”?

A foundation model is a model trained broadly — on a wide variety of text, not on one narrow task — so that it becomes a versatile foundation that can later be specialized. Think of a person with a broad general education: they can later train as a doctor, a lawyer, or a translator, because the general base exists. Fontaine is designed as a foundation model: first broad language training, later fine-tuning toward specific abilities (Chapter 27).

What exactly am I building, concretely?

On your own computer, the first Fontaine is:

  • a small language model — millions (not billions) of adjustable numbers arranged as a Transformer network (Part IV);
  • a tokenizer that turns text into numbers the model can read (Part III);
  • a data pipeline that turns your documents into clean training material (Part II);
  • a trainer that teaches the model by example (Part VI);
  • and an inference engine that lets the trained model generate answers (Part VIII).

Everything is configured by files, runs on one machine, and is small enough to understand completely — which is precisely what makes it a foundation you can later grow.

Honest Limitation

What Fontaine is not: it is not a finished product that understands like a human, and the first version will not out-think commercial assistants. Its intelligence will be modest by design — small data, small compute. Its value is different: it is yours, understandable end-to-end, and architecturally honest about what grows later.

Why This Matters

Every later chapter names its component as part of this layer picture — model, platform, application. When Chapter 38 shows the Trainer or Chapter 40 the inference server, you will know exactly which layer they belong to: the platform, serving the model.

Key Takeaways
  • Fontaine AI = a small language model + the platform that builds and serves it.
  • A model is an engine; an application is the car built around it.
  • Foundation model = broadly trained first, specialized later.
  • The first version is deliberately small, understandable, and yours.
Chapter 2

How Does AI Learn?

Traditional software follows rules a human wrote. Machine learning flips this: humans provide examples, and the machine finds the rules. This chapter explains that flip.

Two ways to tell a computer what to do

Traditional programming

A human writes explicit rules: “if the email contains the word ‘lottery’, mark it as spam.” The programmer must anticipate every situation. Works well when the rules are known and simple; fails when they are subtle, countless, or impossible to write down.

Machine learning

A human provides examples — thousands of emails already labeled spam or not spam — and the machine discovers the distinguishing patterns itself. Works when rules are too complex to write: recognizing faces, understanding language, predicting the next word.

Machine learning is the engineering discipline of getting computers to improve at a task from experience (examples) rather than from hand-written instructions. Language is the classic case where rules fail: no one can write down every rule of grammar, idiom, and meaning — but there are trillions of words of example text.

Neural networks and deep learning, briefly

The machine that does this learning is usually a neural network: a structure of many simple calculation units (neurons) organized in layers, each layer transforming the information a little more. “Deep learning” simply means using many layers in a row — deep stack rather than wide single step. Why layers? Because understanding text seems to happen in stages — letters, then words, then phrases, then meaning — and a stack of layers lets the network build up increasingly abstract patterns, one stage per layer. Chapter 10 opens this up properly; for now, the key image is: many simple units, stacked deep, adjustable by many small dials.

Learning = predict, measure error, correct, repeat

Here is the entire learning loop of machine learning, no mathematics needed yet:

Analogy — practice with feedback

Think of a student learning to throw a basketball. Nobody hands them an equation for the perfect shot. They throw (predict), watch where the ball lands (error), and adjust their stance and force a little (correction). Ten thousand throws later, the skill looks effortless. A neural network trains the same way, except the “ball” is a prediction, and the adjustments happen millions of times per second.

The prediction game a language model plays

Language models learn through one specific game: guess the next word. Show the model:

“The sky is ___”

Early in training the model guesses nearly randomly — perhaps “purple” or “underneath”. The correct answer, taken from real text, is “blue.” The error is measured, the internal settings are nudged toward making “blue” a bit more likely next time, and the next example arrives. After billions of such nudges on real text — news, books, conversations, code — the model has absorbed enough pattern of how language flows to continue almost any sentence plausibly, and, remarkably, to answer questions, because answers are just a very informative kind of continuation.

Beginner Note

“Where does the correct answer come from?” From the data itself. In a text corpus, every word is already followed by another word. “The sky is blue.” contains its own training example: given “The sky is”, the correct next word is “blue”. No human labeling needed — the text labels itself. This is why language models can train on enormous data.

What learning is not

Learning is not a person typing facts into a database, and it is not copying documents. It is tuning: millions of adjustable numbers slowly re-balanced until the network's behavior matches the patterns in the examples. Nothing resembling a sentence is stored anywhere you could point to — which is why the model can say things it never saw, and also why it can be confidently wrong (Chapter 34 returns to this).

Why This Matters

This loop — predict, measure error, correct, repeat — is the skeleton of Chapter 18's training walk-through, the reason optimizers exist (Chapter 20), and the reason GPUs matter (Chapter 16): the loop is simple, but it must run billions of times, and GPUs are machines for doing exactly that.

Key Takeaways
  • Traditional programming = human writes rules; machine learning = machine finds rules from examples.
  • A neural network is many simple units in layers; “deep” means many layers.
  • Learning = predict, measure error, adjust slightly, repeat millions of times.
  • Language models train on “guess the next word” — text is its own answer key.
Chapter 3

What Is a Language Model?

Words, tokens, context, probability — the four ideas that define what a language model is, and what it is not (a lookup table).

Language as a stream of pieces

Before a model can work with text, the text must be broken into small standard pieces called tokens — think of them as word-fragments — and each piece must have a fixed slot in a master list called the vocabulary. If this sounds like slicing a sentence into LEGO bricks and numbering every brick type, you have the right picture. Chapters 7 and 8 are devoted to it; for now, just hold the idea: to a language model, text is a sequence of numbered pieces.

The core job: next-token prediction

A language model does exactly one thing: given a sequence of tokens (the context — everything visible so far), it assigns a probability to every token in its vocabulary, answering “how plausible is each piece as the next one?” Probabilities are numbers between 0 and 1 — how strongly the model believes each option — and they always sum to 1 across all choices, like a raffle where every ticket is sold.

From prediction to conversation

How does a next-token predictor become something that answers questions? Through repetition. Ask “Explain gravity.” — the model predicts a first token, attaches it to the context, predicts the next, attaches it, and so on, one piece at a time, until it produces a stop signal. An “answer” is a long chain of good continuations. This autoregressive loop (each output feeds back as input) is why Chapter 26's generation settings and Chapter 25's inference walkthrough matter: they are all about running this loop well.

What a language model is not: a giant lookup table

A tempting picture is that the model memorized a dictionary of question → answer. It did not — and the difference is crucial:

A lookup table would…
  • only answer questions seen verbatim in training;
  • fail the instant a question is reworded;
  • have no notion of grammar, tone, or length;
  • be uselessly brittle.
A language model actually…
  • learned patterns of how language flows — grammar, style, logic-shaped structure;
  • can produce sentences that never appeared in training;
  • adapts to any context you type, even one invented a minute ago;
  • generalizes: patterns learned in one place apply in another.
Analogy — the jazz musician

A lookup table is a musician with a fixed set of recordings, replayed exactly. A language model is a jazz player: after hearing enormous amounts of music, they internalize the style and can improvise over a tune they have never heard before. They can also hit a wrong note confidently — improvisers do — which is exactly what an AI “hallucination” is.

Generation, in one paragraph

So: tokens from a fixed vocabulary; context is what the model can see; it outputs a probability for each possible next token; a selection rule (Part VIII) picks one; the chosen token joins the context; repeat until a stop signal. That is text generation. Everything else in this book — data pipelines, GPUs, checkpoints — exists to make that one loop good and dependable.

Technical Note — what “model” means physically

Physically, the trained model is: (1) an architecture — the fixed blueprint of layers and connections — plus (2) weights — the millions of learned numbers that fill the blueprint. Fontaine keeps the blueprint in configuration files and the weights in checkpoint files. Losing either makes the model unusable; that is why Chapter 22 guards checkpoints like save-games.

Why This Matters

Part I's picture is now complete: the learning loop (Ch 2) + the prediction game (Ch 3) define the machine Fontaine builds. Part II asks the first practical question: where do the billions of training examples come from — your data.

Key Takeaways
  • A language model assigns a probability to every possible next token given the context.
  • Generation = predict one token, append, repeat — until a stop signal.
  • It is a learned pattern of language flow, not a memorized dictionary of answers.
  • Tokens and vocabulary are the model's alphabet — the subject of Part III.

Chapter 5

Data: The Foundation of Fontaine

A model can only learn patterns that exist in its training data. This chapter is about what training data is, and why quality quietly dominates quantity.

Analogy — the library

A dataset is the library the student studies from. Train a student in a library of romance novels and they will speak like romance novels; give them an encyclopedia and they will sound like one. No library makes the student smarter than the material — the material defines what can be learned. And as with libraries, a thousand excellent books beat a million water-damaged pamphlets.

Quantity vs. quality — the quiet hierarchy

Both matter, but not equally, and not in the way headlines imply:

  1. Quality first. A small, clean, coherent dataset produces a model that writes clearly. A huge dataset full of spam, gibberish, and duplicated junk teaches noise — the model cannot tell “signal” from “pollution,” it just imitates all of it.
  2. Quantity second — but genuinely needed. A model cannot learn the broad flow of language from a hundred pages. Practical floor for anything usable: millions of words; comfortable: hundreds of millions; large open datasets: billions. Chapter 30 puts numbers per model size.
  3. Diversity third. Ten million words of only cooking recipes make a recipe-flavored model. Variety of sources, styles, and topics creates flexible patterns.

What happens if the data is bad?

Concretely, each common failure has a predictable symptom:

This is why the pipeline spends so much effort on the next chapter — and why Chapter 6's rules about deduplication, licensing, and personal data exist as engineering, not bureaucracy.

Honest Limitation — how much data do you need, really?

For a real, general-capability foundation model, the honest answer is “billions of quality tokens,” which at reading speed (about 250 words per minute) would take a person thousands of lifetimes to read. Your 16 GB machine trains happily on millions to hundreds of millions of tokens — enough to produce a genuinely working small model and to learn the entire craft. It is not enough to match commercial assistants, and no config file changes that (Rule 8 of this book's writing rules applies to its own project too).

Why This Matters

Every capability question a user later asks the model is, in a sense, answered by this chapter: the model can only recombine patterns that were present in data like this. Data choices are model-design choices.

Key Takeaways
  • The dataset is the library; the model cannot exceed what its library teaches.
  • Quality > quantity > diversity — in that order of priority, all three needed.
  • Each data failure mode has a predictable model symptom; prevention is cheaper than cure.
  • Licensing and privacy are data-design decisions, made before training, not after.
Chapter 6

Dataset Cleaning and Preparation

Raw text is never ready. Here is the washing machine Fontaine runs it through — every step exists because a specific failure appears if you skip it.

Why “just download everything” fails

Blindly scraping large amounts of internet text feels efficient and is nearly always a mistake. Internet text is roughly: duplicates of other pages, templates and navigation menus, spam, ads, broken encodings that turn characters into noise, scraped personal details, and — increasingly — AI-generated text feeding on itself. Training on that mixture teaches the model all of it. The cleaning stages below are how a responsible pipeline says no.

The human part: dangerous, personal, and borrowed content

  • Personally identifiable information (PII) — names, phone numbers, addresses — can be memorized by the model and leak into outputs. The pipeline can flag suspicious patterns, but the reliable method is reviewing sources before training.
  • Dangerous or inappropriate content — the model will reproduce it. Filtering policy is your decision as the model's owner; it belongs in the cleaning stage, not in hope.
  • Data provenance — “where did each document come from?” — and data licensing — “what does its license permit?” — are recorded in Fontaine's dataset manifest: source, license, language, checksums, creation date. Future-you (or a lawyer, or a collaborator) can trace every training token back to its origin. This is bookkeeping, not legal clearance — engineering can support compliance, never replace it.
Analogy — cooking

Raw data is unsorted groceries straight from the market: some fresh, some rotten, some duplicated, some mislabeled, some you have no receipt for. Washing, trimming, and checking isn't fussiness — it's the difference between a meal and food poisoning. Training is a one-way process: whatever you serve gets baked into the result.

Technical Note — dedup at scale

Exact duplicates are easy: hash each document (a short digital fingerprint) and drop repeats. Near-duplicates (“the same article, reposted with a new title”) need fuzzy techniques such as MinHash, which Fontaine defers to the scaled-up future — the pipeline interface stays identical either way.

Why This Matters

Clean data flows into tokenization (Part III) and then into every training step (Part VI). A dollar of data care here saves a hundred dollars of confusion in Chapter 19 when loss refuses to fall.

Key Takeaways
  • Every cleaning stage maps to a specific failure it prevents — cleaning is diagnosis-driven.
  • Deduplication protects the model from parroting; normalization protects token consistency.
  • PII, harmful content, and licensing are decided by policy in the pipeline, before training.
  • Provenance is recorded in the dataset manifest — traceability is a feature.
Part III

Turning Language into Numbers

Computers compute numbers, not words. Three chapters follow the conversion: text is split into tokens (Ch 7), a tokenizer assigns each a number (Ch 8), and each number is expanded into a rich numeric “meaning profile” called an embedding (Ch 9).

Chapter 7

What Is a Token?

Tokens are the atoms of the model's world. Almost everything surprising about how language models behave — their strengths and their strangeness — starts here.

Why not just feed raw text?

A computer's circuits perform arithmetic on numbers; the letters “c”, “a”, “t” mean nothing to an adder. So text must be converted to numbers. Why not simply convert each letter to its numeric code and be done? Because raw letters carry almost no structure at the point of use: the model would have to re-learn spelling, syllables, and word boundaries from scratch inside every training run, at enormous cost. And why not one number per word? Because language has millions of word forms — including typos, names, numbers, and words in every language — and a table that big is unusable. Tokens are the compromise: pieces bigger than letters, smaller than the dictionary.

Meet the pieces

Tokenization is not the same as splitting by words. Depending on the tokenizer's design, the same sentence may become words, subword fragments, or even characters:

Beginner Note — why subwords are the sweet spot

“unbelievability” is rare, but “un”, “believ” and “ability” appear all over the language. By learning such reusable fragments, a subword tokenizer can express any word — even “Bengali” or a made-up brand name — using a compact vocabulary of perhaps 8,000–64,000 pieces. Rare words cost more tokens; common words cost one. That trade is the whole design.

Byte-level tokenization: the safety net

The deepest fallback is byte-level tokenization. Computers already store text as bytes (numbers 0–255) before it is ever letters. A byte-level tokenizer treats those 256 base values as always-available tokens. Consequence: no input can ever be unreadable — an emoji, a rare script, a binary glitch, a new word — everything decomposes to bytes and remains expressible. Fontaine's production tokenizer is byte-level BPE exactly for this guarantee (Chapter 8).

Honest Limitation — token quirks you will actually notice

Because tokens (not letters) are the unit: counting the letters in “strawberry” is oddly hard for language models; arithmetic with big numbers is clumsy (numbers split into fragments); and non-English text sometimes costs more tokens per idea if the tokenizer's vocabulary is English-heavy. These are tokenizer artifacts, not stupidity — and they are engineering-visible, which is why Fontaine keeps the tokenizer a separate, replaceable component.

Key Takeaways
  • Tokens are the standard pieces the model actually reads — bigger than letters, smaller than all words.
  • Words, subwords, characters, and bytes are granularity choices; subwords are the practical sweet spot.
  • Byte-level fallback guarantees every possible input is representable.
  • Token quirks explain real model quirks (letter counting, number arithmetic, language cost).
Chapter 8

What Is a Tokenizer?

The tokenizer is the translator with two dictionaries: text → numbers when reading, numbers → text when speaking. Small component, huge consequences.

What is it?

A fixed two-way converter: it cuts text into tokens (encoding) and turns token sequences back into text (decoding). It is not learned during model training — it is built beforehand from a sample of your corpus and then frozen.

Why do we need it?

The model computes with numbers. Without a shared, fixed text↔number contract there is no input and no output — and no way to ever decode a generation back into words.

How does it work?

It owns the vocabulary — the master list mapping each token to a token ID (its number). “The sky is” becomes, say, [464, 6766, 318] — just a list of integers the model can read, and the only form it will ever read.

Why does Fontaine treat it as a separate component?

Because token choice affects everything (cost, languages, quirks) but is independent of the model's brain. Keeping it replaceable means trying a Bengali-optimized tokenizer later requires no change to the model architecture — only retraining with the new vocabulary size.

Inside the vocabulary

Special tokens deserve emphasis: they are reserved vocabulary slots with special jobs — marking document boundaries, signaling start/end, providing padding. The “unknown” slot is the escape hatch for characters the tokenizer never met — though a byte-level tokenizer (Ch 7) almost never needs it, because bytes cover everything.

How Fontaine builds one — and the choices

A tokenizer is trained (statistics, not neural networks): feed it a sample of your corpus and an optional target vocabulary size; it discovers the most useful fragments. Fontaine ships two kinds:

Analogy — the LEGO ledger

Think of the tokenizer as the clerk who invented your LEGO kit: they looked at thousands of models, decided which brick shapes appear most (plus a few special “connector” pieces — the special tokens), and printed one fixed catalog with a part number for every shape. Building (encoding) means looking up part numbers; reading a finished model (decoding) means looking parts up by number. The catalog is frozen before construction begins — the model never rewrites it.

Why a tokenizer is not the AI's brain

The tokenizer is pure bookkeeping — no learning happens inside it at generation time, and it contains no language understanding. It cannot paraphrase, translate, or answer. Swap the metaphorical brain out and the clerk still works. This matters because the tokenizer shapes what the model can easily learn (how meaningfully text is chunked) without being any of the intelligence.

Technical Note — sequence length

Models process a fixed maximum number of tokens per pass — the sequence length or context window. Fontaine's config sets it (tiny: 256 tokens; bigger configs: 512–1024+). Every position the model can attend to costs computation, which is why context length is a first-class scaling knob (Ch 30–31).

Key Takeaways
  • Encoding: text → token IDs. Decoding: IDs → text. Both directions use one frozen vocabulary.
  • Special tokens (pad/bos/eos/unk) are reserved IDs with control jobs — eos is how a model stops.
  • Character, BPE, and Unigram are interchangeable strategies behind one interface.
  • Sequence length is a tokenizer-visible limit that becomes the model's context window.
Chapter 9

Embeddings

Token IDs are labels, not meanings — the number 901 is just “row 901.” Embeddings give each token a rich numeric profile that can actually carry meaning.

What is a vector, quickly?

A vector is just a list of numbers: [0.2, -1.3, 0.8, …]. The useful magic is that lists of numbers can encode relationships through geometry. Picture a map: every city is a point, and “distance” means something — nearby cities are related. Do the same with meanings: place each token as a point in a large space (Fontaine uses hundreds of dimensions — far more room than a 2-D map), so that tokens used in similar ways sit near each other.

What is it?

An embedding is the learned vector attached to each token. Fontaine stores them in an embedding table: one row per vocabulary entry, each row hidden_size numbers wide (256 for Fontaine Tiny, 512–768 in bigger configs).

Why do we need it?

A bare ID can only be “equal” or “different” — 901 shares nothing with 902 even if they mean nearly the same thing. A vector can be close to, between, and blended with others — the raw material for reasoning by similarity.

How does it work?

Looking up token 901 is literally: take row 901 of the table. The learning part: during training these rows are adjusted like every other parameter, so that rows used in similar contexts drift toward each other. Nobody programs “cat near kitten” — usage history carves the geometry.

Why does Fontaine use learned embeddings (not a fixed dictionary)?

Hand-built meaning tables (like early “word vectors”) bake in someone's assumptions and don't know your corpus's special words. Learning them from your data lets the geometry match the language Fontaine will actually see — including your domain's vocabulary.

Analogy — flavor profiles

Imagine describing every ingredient by a long taste list: sweetness, saltiness, acidity, umami, heat… [0.9, 0.0, 0.6, 0.1, 0.0] might be a lemon. Recipes become blends of profiles. Two ingredients with similar lists substitute well; the list — not the name — carries what matters. An embedding is a flavor profile for a token, except the “taste dimensions” are hundreds and learned automatically.

Beginner Note — but what do the numbers mean?

Individually, nothing interpretable — dimension 137 is not “animateness.” Meaning lives in the pattern across all dimensions, like how no single pixel of a photograph is “the cat,” yet the whole picture is. The model reads whole vectors at once; humans read the geometry between them.

Technical Note — embedding counts

The embedding table has vocab_size × hidden_size numbers — e.g. 8,192 × 512 ≈ 4.2 million for a small setup. When the output layer shares this table (Fontaine ties them by default), the same rows both read tokens in and score tokens out — a parameter saving that matters at small scale.

Why This Matters

Embeddings are the interface between Part III and Part IV: text has become numbers with geometric meaning, and the Transformer's attention (Chapter 12) is the machinery that lets these vectors interact. Everything downstream operates on embeddings, never on text.

Key Takeaways
  • Embedding = learned vector per token, stored as one row of a lookup table.
  • Similar usage → nearby vectors: meaning becomes geometry the network can compute with.
  • Individual dimensions have no human-readable meaning; the pattern does.
  • Lookup is trivial; the geometry is entirely learned from your corpus.

Chapter 10

Neural Networks

Before the Transformer, the plain neural network: neurons, layers, weights, parameters, activations, and the forward pass. Intuition first — mathematics is optional here.

A neuron: a tiny weighted vote

A neuron is a very small decision unit. It receives some input numbers, gives each one an importance score, adds everything up, and reports the result. That's all. A useful mental model: a weighted vote — some inputs shout, some whisper, the neuron sums the votes and passes the total upward. “Learning” means slowly changing the importance scores.

Layers: stages of refinement

One neuron is nearly powerless; thousands arranged in a layer, and layers stacked deep, are formidable. Each layer transforms the whole representation a step further: early layers might pick up letter- and word-level patterns, middle layers phrases and relationships, late layers full meaning-shaped structure. Nobody assigns these roles — they emerge from training, layer by layer.

What are weights and parameters?

Weights are those importance scores — the adjustable numbers inside the network. Parameters is the umbrella word for all adjustable numbers: weights, biases (small offsets added before squashing), and embedding tables. “A 7-billion-parameter model” means 7 billion of these numbers, each individually tuned by training.

Analogy — the mixing desk

Picture a recording studio mixing desk with millions of sliders. The song (input text) flows through; every slider's position subtly shapes the sound. Training is an automated engineer nudging sliders after every playback, guided by how “wrong” the sound was. The analogy ends here: the sliders don't store human-like knowledge — only a configuration that, as a whole, produces good predictions.

What is the forward pass?

Pushing input through all layers to get output is a forward pass. For a language model: embeddings go in one end, next-token probabilities come out the other. One pass per example — and during training, one pass per batch, millions of times. This is why speed at simple arithmetic is the hardware bottleneck (Ch 16).

Why so many parameters?

Capacity. Language has astronomical variety; a small network's pattern library is too coarse to capture it, like reproducing a photograph with 12 pixels. Parameters are the pixels of learned knowledge — hence billions in frontier models, millions in Fontaine Tiny.

Beginner Note — where does the knowledge live?

Nowhere you can point to. It is distributed: the “understanding of grammar” is a property of how millions of numbers interact, not a file or a region. Like a smoke ring in air — remove any few particles and it persists; it is a pattern of the whole. This is why models generalize (reuse patterns in new contexts) and why they can't quote training documents reliably (no document is stored).

Why This Matters

The Transformer in the next chapter is nothing more than a very well-chosen way of arranging these layers. Every later mention of “the model” means: this stack, with these parameters, doing forward passes.

Key Takeaways
  • Neuron = weighted vote; layer = a stage of refinement; depth = stacked stages.
  • Parameters are all adjustable numbers — the “sliders” training tunes.
  • Forward pass = input to output through the whole stack; the model's only mode of operation.
  • Knowledge is distributed across parameters, not stored as documents or facts.
Chapter 11

The Transformer

The architecture behind essentially every modern language model — including Fontaine. What it is, why it won, and a tour of its parts (each expanded in the next three chapters).

The problem with the older way

Before 2017, language models mostly processed text the way people read a rubric: word by word, left to right, carrying a running “memory” (recurrent networks — RNNs). Two fatal flaws emerged at scale:

  • Memory fading. By the end of a paragraph, information from the beginning had been squeezed through every intermediate step and largely evaporated. Long-range connections — the heart of language — were nearly lost.
  • No parallelism. Step 21 can't start before step 20 finishes. Training crawled, because the machine's thousands of parallel processors sat mostly idle.

The Transformer (paper title: “Attention Is All You Need”, 2017) abolished the running memory. Its move: let every position look at every other position directly, in one step, and learn how much to look at each. That mechanism is attention — Chapter 12 — and it fixed both flaws at once.

Recurrent (old)
  • one word at a time, carrying a fading summary
  • distant words practically invisible
  • strictly sequential → slow training
Transformer (now)
  • all positions visible to each other in one step
  • direct long-range connections, strength learned
  • fully parallel → scales to GPUs beautifully

The parts of a Transformer block

The Transformer is a stack of identical blocks. Each block contains (details in Chapters 12–14):

Residual connectionsan information express-lane that lets inputs bypass each sub-step unchanged14

Why Fontaine chose the Transformer — and why decoder-only

Why a Transformer at all?

Because the alternatives lose on the two axes that matter most: long-range language structure (attention wins) and hardware efficiency (parallelism wins). Choosing a Transformer is choosing the ecosystem the whole industry — datasets, code, hardware, papers — has optimized around.

Why decoder-only?

Transformers come in three styles: encoder-only (understands text — search, classification), encoder–decoder (transforms one text into another — translation), and decoder-only (continues text — generation). Fontaine's goal is generation, and the single decoder stack is the simplest architecture that does it, trains efficiently, and matches how assistants like GPT work.

Why not a RNN / LSTM after all?

Simpler per step, but memory fades and training can't parallelize — disqualifying at any serious scale. (Newer “state-space” models revisit this trade-off; Fontaine's config reserves a slot for future architectures, but the Transformer is the proven default.)

Why This Matters

The block is Fontaine's Lego brick: Chapter 15's full model is literally “embeddings → N blocks → output head.” Understanding one block (Ch 12–14) means understanding every size Fontaine will ever have.

Key Takeaways
  • Transformers replaced recurrence with attention: direct, learned connections between any positions.
  • Parallelism is why they scale on GPUs; direct access is why they handle long-range language.
  • A block = attention + feed-forward + residual + normalization; the model is a stack of blocks.
  • Decoder-only = the generation-friendly style — Fontaine's choice.
Chapter 12

Attention

The most important mechanism in modern AI — explained with one famous sentence, then gently made precise. Take your time here; the rest of the book leans on it.

The sentence that shows why attention exists

“The animal didn't cross the road because it was tired.”

What does “it” refer to? Obviously — to us — the animal. But a machine sees only the word “it”, which could grammatically refer to the road. To resolve it, the model must look backward through the sentence, gather the other words, and decide which one matters. And now the famous twist: change the ending to “because it was too wide” and “it” silently becomes the road. The referent is not a rule; it is a relationship computed from context. That computation is attention.

What is self-attention?

Self-attention lets every position in the sequence examine every other position (in Fontaine's decoder: every earlier position) and pull in whatever is relevant, with learned strength. “Self” means the sequence attends to itself — no external database involved.

Why do we need it?

Meaning is relational. “It”, “bank”, “not”, “only” — their interpretation depends on other words, sometimes hundreds of tokens away. A model without direct cross-position access cannot compute those relationships reliably.

How does it work — queries, keys, values?

Every position's embedding is projected into three roles (all learned linear maps): a Query (“what am I looking for?”), a Key (“what do I offer?”), and a Value (“what I'll hand over if you pick me”). Keep reading — the analogy below makes this concrete — then Figure 12.1 shows the flow.

Why does Fontaine use it?

Because language understanding is relationship computation, and attention is the mechanism that computes it in parallel with learned strength — the core of the Chapter 11 decision.

Analogy — the library research desk

You (a word) walk in with a research query: “I'm the word it — I need to know what noun I stand for.” Every other word in the sentence has a placard — its key — advertising what it offers: “animal, noun, subject.” You compare your query to every placard; the matches feel stronger or weaker; those match strengths are your attention scores. You then take a blended copy of what the best-matching words offer — their values — weighted by match strength. You walk out enriched: your representation now literally contains “animal-ness.” Every word does this simultaneously — that's self-attention.

Making it precise (gently)

The whole mechanism in one line — read it as the library story in symbols:

Attention(Q, K, V) = softmax(Q KT / √d) V
Q, K, Vthe matrices of Queries, Keys, Values — each position contributes one of each, built from its embedding by learned weight matrices
Q KT“compare every query against every key” — a table of match scores for all position pairs
√da normalizer (d = the vectors' size) that keeps scores from growing too sharp simply because vectors are long
softmaxconverts raw scores into positive weights summing to 1 — the raffle tickets of Ch 3
× V“blend the values, weighted by the weights” — the enriched output

Multi-head attention — several specialists at once

One attention pass captures one kind of relationship. Language needs many: grammar roles, pronoun references, topic links, numbers and their units. So the model runs several attention computations in parallel — heads — each with its own learned Q/K/V projections, then merges their outputs. Heads are not assigned topics; they specialize spontaneously during training (researchers have found heads that track pronouns, others that follow syntax). Fontaine Tiny uses 4 heads; Medium uses 12.

Technical Note — grouped-query attention (GQA)

During generation, every past token's Keys and Values must be kept in memory (the KV cache — Ch 25). GQA lets several query heads share one Key/Value head, shrinking that memory by the sharing ratio with almost no quality loss. Fontaine uses it (Tiny: 4 query heads share 2 KV heads) — a small model practicing the trick that keeps frontier inference affordable.

Honest Limitation — the cost of “look at everything”

Attention compares every position with every other: double the context, roughly quadruple the comparison work. This quadratic growth is why longer contexts cost disproportionately more (Ch 31) — the single most important constraint on the Transformer's economics.

Key Takeaways
  • Self-attention computes, for every position, a learned-weighted blend of information from other positions.
  • Query/Key/Value = “what I seek / what I advertise / what I hand over” — all learned.
  • Softmax turns match scores into blending weights that sum to one.
  • Multiple heads = multiple relationship specialists in parallel; GQA makes the KV memory cheaper.
Chapter 13

Positional Information

Attention treats a sentence as a bag of positions — order-blind by design. But order is meaning. Here is how the model is told where everything sits.

“Dog bites man” vs. “Man bites dog”

Same two nouns, same verb — different universe of meaning, and the difference is pure position. Here's the subtle problem: attention, as built, compares token contents and is blind to where they sit. Shuffle the words and attention scores would barely change. The model needs position injected as a first-class signal — positional encoding — or word order becomes invisible.

What is RoPE?

RoPE (Rotary Position Embedding) is Fontaine's scheme: rather than adding a position number to each token, it rotates each position's Query and Key vectors by an angle proportional to how far along the sequence they sit.

Analogy — the clock hands

Each token's vector is a clock hand; its position is how far the hand has rotated. When two hands are compared, what matters is the angle between them — i.e., how many steps apart the words are. “Distance” falls out of geometry instead of being stored.

Why rotation gives relative distance?

Rotating position 5 by 3 steps and position 8 by 3 steps keeps their angle difference identical. So attention scores naturally depend on relative separation — “the verb is 2 words before the noun” — which transfers across the whole sentence.

Why did Fontaine choose RoPE?

It's the modern standard (Llama-class models use it), it needs no learned position table (so nothing overflows when contexts get longer), and it has one dial — rope_theta — for stretching context length later. Chapter 30's scaling story leans on exactly that dial.

Why not simpler options?

Add position numbers to embeddings
  • simple, works — the original Transformer's choice
  • needs a learned slot for every position: position 1,001 is outside the table
  • absolute positions generalize poorly
Rotate (RoPE)
  • no learned position table — positions are computed, unbounded
  • relative distance built in
  • context can be extended by tuning theta, not retraining from scratch

Context length, made concrete

The context window is the maximum number of tokens the model can relate at once — the width of its “field of view.” Fontaine's configs: Tiny 256 tokens (roughly a paragraph or two), Small 512, Medium 1024. Beyond the window, tokens are simply outside what this pass can see — RoPE has no angles for positions it was never sized for. Chapter 30 shows how window growth is one of the four big scaling dimensions.

Beginner Note — attention also has a “no looking ahead” rule

During training, the model predicts each next token from the ones before it — so position i is only allowed to attend to positions ≤ i. This causal mask is what makes one training pass produce a prediction for every position at once, and it's why generation feels like writing with a one-way mirror.

Key Takeaways
  • Attention is order-blind by construction; position must be injected explicitly.
  • RoPE encodes position as rotation, so attention naturally sees relative distance.
  • Context window = how many positions exist; RoPE's theta is the scaling dial.
  • A causal mask keeps the model honest about not peeking at the future.
Chapter 14

Feed-Forward Networks and Transformer Blocks

Attention gathers; the feed-forward network thinks. This chapter assembles the full block — gathering + thinking + two safety mechanisms — and explains why both halves are needed.

Two different jobs

Attention — the gathering step

Moves information between positions: “it” pulls in “animal.” It is communication across the sequence.

Crosses positions; identical machinery for every pair.

Feed-forward network (FFN) — the thinking step

Processes each position individually: takes the freshly enriched representation and transforms it through a small two-layer network — expanding it to a much wider inner size (Fontaine Tiny: 256 → 768), applying a non-linearity, and compressing back.

No cross-position talk; pure per-position computation.

Why both? Gathered information is not yet used information. After “it” absorbs “animal,” something must compute what that means for this position's next-token prediction — refine, combine, interpret. The FFN is where that per-position computation happens; researchers associate a large share of a model's factual pattern storage with FFN layers. Remove the FFN and attention becomes a courier with nowhere to file the letters; remove attention and every word thinks alone in a soundproof room.

Inside the FFN: the SwiGLU pattern

Fontaine uses a modern “gated” FFN (SwiGLU — also used by Llama-class models). Read it as: one pathway proposes content (up), a second pathway scores how much of it to let through (gate), and the result is compressed back down (down). The gate's learned control is what makes the layer selective — expressive where the pattern matters, quiet where it doesn't.

Why the two safety mechanisms exist

Residual connections — why?

Each sub-step adds its contribution to the stream instead of overwriting it: an express-lane where the original signal flows untouched, and each layer deposits an increment. Without this, deep stacks smother their own signal within a few layers — gradients fade before reaching early layers, and 12+ layer models simply fail to train. With it, depth stacks reliably.

Normalization — why?

Numbers passing through millions of operations can drift — inflate or shrink — until training destabilizes. Normalization (Fontaine uses RMSNorm, a leaner cousin of LayerNorm) re-scales each token's vector to a standard size before each sub-step. It is the steady hand that keeps a 12-layer stack as numerically calm as a 2-layer one.

Beginner Note — “why layers at all?”

Could one giant layer do everything? In principle more depth means more composition — patterns built on patterns built on patterns, like grammar inside clauses inside paragraphs. Depth gives the network conceptual headroom; the residual+normalization pair is what makes that headroom usable. That's the real answer to “why do we need layers”: not size, but stages of composition.

Why This Matters

The block is now complete — and it is the only thing that repeats inside the model. Chapter 15's “architecture diagram” is this block, stacked N times, with an entrance (embeddings) and an exit (the language-model head). Part IV is the whole brain.

Key Takeaways
  • Attention communicates across positions; the FFN computes within a position. Both are required.
  • Gated FFNs (SwiGLU) propose-and-select content per position.
  • Residual connections preserve signal through depth; normalization keeps numbers stable.
  • The block repeats — N blocks = the model's depth — so one understood block scales to any size.

Chapter 16

Why Fontaine Starts Small

Starting small is not lowered ambition — it is the engineering strategy that makes everything later possible. Here is the accounting behind that choice.

What a small model buys you

  • Experimentation speed. A Fontaine Tiny run takes minutes. Every idea — new data mix, cleaned text, changed setting — can be tested the same hour. On a large model the same test is a multi-day experiment; you would make ten discoveries a year instead of ten a day.
  • Full visibility. A 4-million-parameter model can be inspected, probed, overfit on purpose (the classic “can it memorize a tiny set?” sanity check), and debugged end-to-end. Understanding compounds on small models, then transfers.
  • Cheap mistakes. Every learner burns through failed runs. Failed runs that cost minutes are education; failed runs that cost thousands of GPU-hours are disasters.
  • Correctness of the pipeline. Most “the model is bad” problems are actually data, config, or evaluation problems. A small model surfaces them immediately instead of hiding them behind scale.

Chapter 18

What Does Training Actually Mean?

One single training step, dissected into its nine beats. Everything else in training is this step, repeated.

The beats that deserve explanation

Steps 1–2: the example arrives pre-cut. The dataset was tokenized and packed into fixed-length token windows back in Part II — the loader just hands the model the next window. Each window doubles as question and answer key: every position's answer is simply the token one step to its right. One window of 256 tokens = 256 mini-examples at once. This is the quiet genius of next-token training: no labeling team required.

Steps 3–4: the forward pass (Ch 15). A batch of windows flows through together — parallelism the GPU exists for. The model, still untrained and wrong, outputs probabilities at every position.

Steps 5–6: compare and measure (Ch 19). For each position: how much probability did the model give the token that actually came next? Give the true next token 80% and the error is small; give it 0.001% and the error is enormous. All positions' errors condense into one number: the loss.

Step 7: gradients. A gradient is, for every single parameter, the answer to: “if you nudged me up, would the error grow or shrink, and how strongly?” It is computed automatically by running the whole network backwards — backpropagation. Picture the mixing desk of Chapter 10: after each playback, every slider gets a tiny note saying “up a little” or “down a little.” Analogy limit: sliders are nudged independently; gradients are computed jointly through the network's whole structure — that's what backpropagation is for.

Step 8: the update (Ch 20). The optimizer takes all millions of “which way” notes and applies a carefully-sized nudge to each parameter. Too big a nudge = overshooting; too small = training that never ends. The whole craft lives in sizing that nudge.

Beginner Note — why “slightly”?

Because one example is noisy. A single training window might be unusual; reacting strongly to it would wreck the model on all the normal ones. Small, repeated, averaged corrections are how the model settles toward patterns true across all the data — the same reason a student doesn't rebuild their worldview after one surprising quiz question.

Why This Matters

Chapters 19–22 each zoom into one beat of this figure: loss (5–6), optimizer (7–8), repetition schedule (9), and saving (between repetitions). When a later chapter says “step”, this figure is what happens.

Key Takeaways
  • One step = forward pass → loss → gradients → parameter nudge → repeat.
  • Each token window trains every position at once — text is its own answer key.
  • Gradients are per-parameter “which direction reduces error” signals, computed by backpropagation.
  • Updates are deliberately small; learning comes from accumulation, not leaps.
Chapter 19

Loss

Training's only compass. What the single number means, how it's computed, and the two flavors — training and validation — that tell truth from wishful thinking.

What loss measures

Loss is a single number answering: “how surprised was the model by the truth?” For each position, the model assigned some probability to the token that actually came next. If it gave the true token 90%, mild surprise; 1 in a million, enormous surprise. Language models use cross-entropy, which is essentially “negative log of the assigned probability,” averaged over positions.

Simplified cross-entropy, for one prediction:

loss ≈ −log( probability assigned to the correct token )
probabilitywhat the model gave the token that actually came next (0 to 1)
logturns tiny probabilities into big numbers — being 1-in-a-million wrong hurts far more than being 90%-right is rewarded
minusflips it so that “less surprise” = smaller loss. Perfect confidence in truth → loss 0; uniform guessing over 1,000 tokens → loss ≈ log(1000) ≈ 6.9

Why the equation matters: it defines what “wrong” means for training — and it punishes confident wrongness much harder than hesitant near-misses.

Training loss vs. validation loss — the tale of two datasets

  • Training loss is measured on data the model is currently learning from. It always trends down eventually — the model can, given enough capacity, simply get used to its own material.
  • Validation loss is measured on held-out data — a slice of your corpus the model never trains on. It answers the only question that matters: “is the model learning patterns that transfer?” Fontaine's pipeline carves out this slice automatically (a few percent of documents) precisely so this measurement exists.
Honest Limitation — lower loss is not “better AI”

Loss measures next-token prediction on your data distribution — nothing else. A model can have beautiful loss and still be unhelpful, biased, or confidently wrong on facts. Loss is a compass for training, not a certificate of intelligence. That's why Part VII exists: perplexity, benchmarks, and human checks all sit on top of loss.

Why This Matters

Every later quality claim — “the model is learning”, “overfitting”, “perplexity of 2.5” — decomposes into numbers defined here. Loss is to training what the speedometer and fuel gauge are to driving.

Key Takeaways
  • Loss = average surprise at the true next token (cross-entropy); lower = better prediction.
  • Training loss reports on seen data; validation loss on held-out data is the real exam.
  • Diverging curves diagnose overfitting long before outputs look broken.
  • Loss is necessary — never sufficient — evidence of quality.
Chapter 20

Optimizers

Gradients say “which way”; the optimizer decides “how far, and how cleverly.” Meet gradient descent, the learning rate, AdamW, and weight decay.

What is an optimizer?

The algorithm that converts gradients into parameter updates each step. Gradients only give direction-and-urgency; the optimizer adds strategy — step size, memory of past steps, and regularization — deciding the final nudge applied to every one of the millions of parameters.

Why do parameters need to change at all?

A freshly initialized model is random noise — its predictions are meaningless. The only path from noise to skill is thousands of guided corrections. No updates, no learning; the loss would sit flat forever.

Gradient descent — the core idea

Imagine the loss as a hilly landscape where your location is the current parameter values and altitude is error. Gradients point uphill; gradient descent walks downhill in small steps. Every training step is one footstep.

Why Fontaine uses AdamW

Plain descent uses one stride size for everything. Adam(W) keeps a running memory per parameter — how consistent the gradient has been, and how large — so frequent, stable directions get confident steps and noisy directions get cautious ones. The “W” adds weight decay. It is the field's proven default, and the tiny extra memory it costs was budgeted in Chapter 17.

Gradient descent in its simplest form — one parameter, one step:

new value = old value − learning rate × gradient
gradientwhich direction increases the error (so we subtract — walk downhill)
learning ratehow big a step to take — the single most sensitive dial in training
new valuethe parameter after this step; AdamW modifies how the rate is applied per parameter, not this shape
Analogy — hiking downhill in fog

You can't see the valley; you can only feel the slope under your boots (the gradient). The learning rate is your stride: too long and you leap across the valley and up the other wall (loss explodes); too short and you starve before arriving (training crawls). AdamW is a hiking style with memory: on smooth, consistently-sloping ground you stride out; on treacherous, jittery ground you take careful steps — per slider, not globally.

The learning rate schedule: warmup and cosine decay

Fontaine doesn't use one constant learning rate. It warms up — starting tiny and growing over the first steps (Adam's statistics are unreliable before they accumulate) — then decays along a cosine curve toward a small floor as training ends: big exploring strides early, careful settling strides late. These three numbers (peak rate, warmup length, decay shape) are plain config values in Chapter 36.

Weight decay — the anti-bloat setting

Weight decay gently shrinks parameters toward zero every step unless gradients actively argue to keep them large. Effect: the model prefers the simplest pattern that fits the data instead of brittle, overgrown ones — a light constant anti-overfitting pressure. Fontaine applies it to the big weight matrices but exempts biases and normalization parameters (tiny values that shouldn't be suppressed), which is the standard refinement.

Honest Limitation — no optimizer rescues bad training

A better optimizer cannot fix wrong data, a too-small model, or a learning rate set carelessly. AdamW with a sensible schedule is the default because it's robust, not because it's magic. Most training failures in self-built projects are data or learning-rate failures wearing an optimizer costume.

Key Takeaways
  • Optimizer = strategy layer turning “which way” (gradients) into “how far” (updates).
  • Learning rate is the stride length — too big diverges, too small crawls; warmup + cosine decay tame it.
  • AdamW adds per-parameter memory and weight decay — the robust modern default.
  • Weight decay keeps patterns simple; it is pressure, not salvation.

Batches and the 16 GB rescue: gradient accumulation

Bigger batches train more smoothly, but Chapter 17 showed memory is the wall. Gradient accumulation is the rescue: run several small micro-batches, adding up their gradients without updating, then apply one combined update. The model experiences the learning signal of a large batch while only ever holding a micro-batch in memory. Effective batch size = micro-batch × accumulation steps — a multiplication you configure, not new code.

Beginner Note — how many steps is a training run?

Fontaine counts steps, not epochs: “train for 5,000 steps” means 5,000 updates regardless of dataset size. Rough sense of scale: Tiny on a small corpus — hundreds to thousands of steps, minutes to hours on your machine; the largest frontier runs — hundreds of thousands of steps on tens of thousands of GPUs for months. Same loop; the schedule is the difference.

Why This Matters

The loop plus intervals is exactly what Fontaine's training config describes (Ch 36): steps, batch size, accumulation, intervals, seed. Once you can read Figure 21.1, the config file reads like a sentence.

Key Takeaways
  • Step = one learning cycle; batch = parallel examples per cycle; epoch = one pass over all data.
  • Checkpoints and evaluations are interval-driven side effects of the same loop.
  • Gradient accumulation gives big-batch learning on small-batch memory.
  • Runs are measured in steps — from thousands (yours) to hundreds of thousands (frontier).
Chapter 22

Checkpoints

Training runs for hours, days, longer. Checkpoints make that survivable — crashes, mistakes, and all.

Analogy — saving the game

A checkpoint is a save-game file: not just “where the player stands” but the complete world state — inventory, clock, random-encounter seed — so loading puts you back exactly, as if nothing happened. Fontaine's checkpoints earn the name by saving the RNG state too: resume continues the same data order, the same dropout dice, the same stream of randomness.

What is in a Fontaine checkpoint?
  • model weights — the parameters as they stand
  • optimizer state — AdamW's per-parameter memory (losing it would restart learning clumsily)
  • scheduler & scaler state — where the learning-rate schedule stands
  • RNG states — every random stream, for exact resume
  • metadata — step count, configuration snapshot, tokenizer & dataset versions, code version, and a checksum of every file
Why so much — isn't the model enough?

The model alone lets you use it. Only the full state lets you continue it. AdamW's memory is the cruelest loss: without it, resuming works but stumbles like a sprinter told to keep running after amnesia. The metadata is why Chapter 35's “checkpoint is self-describing” claim is true — inference later reads the stored config and rebuilds the exact architecture.

How does resume work?

fontaine train --resume <checkpoint-folder>: load everything, restore the random streams, and continue stepping — metrics history included. To you it's one flag; underneath, it's the reason checkpoint files carry hashes and version stamps.

Why versioning and integrity matter

A checkpoint written by an older format must never load as garbage — so the format carries a version number, and loaders refuse unknown versions instead of guessing. Every file is checksummed; a silently corrupted file (a bad disk sector mid-run) is detected at load, loudly, not discovered as mysteriously broken training hours later.

Best, latest, and the pruning rhythm

Fontaine keeps latest (for resume) and best (the checkpoint with the lowest validation loss seen so far) as named pointers, plus the few most recent — deleting older ones automatically, because each checkpoint is large (for Small, roughly 28 M parameters × 16 bytes + activations-free extras ≈ hundreds of MB). The best pointer is what you hand to evaluation and inference; latest is what you hand back to the trainer after a crash.

Technical Note — atomic writes

A checkpoint is many files; a crash during saving must never leave a half-written set that looks valid. Fontaine writes each file to a temporary name and renames it into place only when complete, then writes the pointers — so a checkpoint directory is always either fully valid or obviously absent, never deceptively broken.

Key Takeaways
  • Checkpoints = save-games: model + optimizer + schedule + randomness + provenance.
  • The model file serves inference; only the full state serves true resume.
  • Version stamps and checksums turn silent corruption into loud errors.
  • Latest resumes; best gets evaluated and shipped; pruning keeps disk sane.

Beginner Note — perplexity in one paragraph

Perplexity re-expresses loss as an intuition: “the model behaves as if it were choosing among N equally likely options at each step.” Perplexity 100 = “lost among 100 candidates”; perplexity 3 = “basically sees the next word coming.” It is just the exponential of loss — same information, friendlier units. High benchmark scores can come from training-data contamination, narrow test styles, or outright overfitting to popular question sets. A benchmark is one opinion, formatted as a number. Cross-check with your own tasks and your own eyes before believing any score — including scores for commercial models.

  • Perplexity = loss in “choosing among N options” units.
  • Evaluation is a pluggable registry, not a hard-coded report.
  • Every automated score needs a final human read of real outputs.
Chapter 24

Overfitting and Underfitting

The two classic failure modes of any learning system — one memorizes, one never learns — and the practical dials that fix each.

Overfitting — memorizing the textbook

The model clings to the exact training examples — including their noise — instead of the patterns behind them. Symptom: training loss keeps falling while validation loss flattens or rises. Looks like: outputs that parrot training phrases, then fall apart on anything new.

Analogy: a student who memorized last year's exam answers word-for-word and panics when a question is rephrased.

Underfitting — never understood the textbook

The model is too small, trained too briefly, or starved of data to capture even the patterns. Symptom: training loss and validation loss both stuck high; outputs barely better than babble.

Analogy: a student who skimmed one page and now can't answer even the original exam.

The practical dials

If…Turn these knobsWhy it helps
Overfittingmore (and more varied) data · fewer steps / early stopping · weight decay up · smaller modelreduce memorization capacity and time; reward simpler patterns
Underfittingmore steps · bigger model · better data · check the learning rate isn't tinygive the model capacity, time, and signal to find the pattern
Beginner Note — the deliberate overfit test

Paradoxically, the fastest health check for your whole pipeline is to overfit a tiny dataset on purpose: can the model memorize a few sentences? If it can't, something is genuinely broken — data wiring, loss, gradients, learning rate. If it can, then train properly and watch the validation valley. Fontaine's test suite does exactly this, which is why its green ticks mean so much.

Why This Matters

Chapter 19's two curves and this chapter's valley are the daily dashboard of every training run you will ever do, at every scale. Reading them correctly is the single most-used skill in applied AI.

Key Takeaways
  • Overfitting = memorizing (validation rises); underfitting = never learning (both stay high).
  • The validation curve's valley marks the checkpoint worth keeping.
  • Fixes are dial turns: data/steps/decay for overfit; capacity/steps/rate for underfit.
  • Deliberate overfitting of a tiny set is a pipeline health check, not a bug.

Chapter 25

Inference

Teaching a model and using a model are different jobs with different engineering. Here is what happens when someone actually asks Fontaine a question.

Training vs. inference — two different modes

Training
  • parameters change every step
  • needs the correct answers (the data)
  • memory-heavy: gradients, optimizer, activations (Ch 17)
  • hours to months
Inference
  • parameters frozen — pure use
  • needs only the question
  • lighter: one forward pass at a time, plus a KV cache
  • seconds, forever repeatable

Inference is “using the trained model to answer rather than teaching it” — a student in an exam hall instead of a classroom. All of Chapter 18's machinery (loss, gradients, optimizer) stands down; only the forward pass runs.

What happens when you type “Explain gravity.”

The KV cache — inference's one clever trick

Naively, generating token 500 would recompute attention over all 499 previous tokens from scratch — every single step. The KV cache avoids this: each position's Keys and Values (Ch 12) are computed exactly once, stored, and reused. Each new token then costs attention against a memory, not a recomputation of history. This is why Chapter 12's grouped-query attention matters: GQA shrinks the cache, which shrinks the memory bill of every generated word.

Beginner Note — does the model learn from our conversation?

No — not during inference. Your messages live in the context window as tokens; when the conversation ends (or the window overflows), the model remembers nothing. “Learning from users” in commercial products is a separate, deliberate training process — never an automatic side effect of chatting.

Why This Matters

Inference is where the platform's other components converge: the checkpoint (Ch 22) supplies frozen weights, the tokenizer both opens and closes the loop, and the config file (Ch 36) carries the generation settings the next chapter explains. Chapter 40 shows how this single-user flow becomes a multi-user service.

Key Takeaways
  • Inference = frozen model, forward passes only; training's machinery stands down.
  • “Explain gravity” = encode → prefill (build KV cache) → decode loop → eos → decode text.
  • The KV cache reuses per-position Keys/Values so generation stays cheap per token.
  • No learning happens while chatting — context window only.
Chapter 26

Text Generation

At every step the model outputs probabilities for the whole vocabulary. Generation settings decide how to choose — and that choice shapes the personality of the output.

Deterministic vs. probabilistic

Two philosophies. Deterministic (greedy): always take the highest-probability token — same input, same output, every time; safe, but can fall into repetitive loops, like a speaker who always uses their favorite sentence. Probabilistic: sample from the distribution — sometimes pick the second or third choice — trading a little predictability for variety and liveliness. Both are one config value away in Fontaine (temperature: 0 makes sampling fully deterministic).

Because sampling is random — the dice of Chapter 12's raffle, rolled per token. Fontaine accepts a seed: fix the seed and the dice become deterministic, giving the same output every time. Honest Limitation — settings don't create ability

No temperature rescues a model that doesn't know the answer; no top-p makes reasoning appear. Sampling controls style and variety over the model's actual competence. Quality still comes from data, training, and scale — the settings are the last 5%, lovingly polished.

Key Takeaways
  • Generation = repeated choosing from probabilities; settings shape the choosing, not the model.
  • Temperature = risk dial; top-k/top-p = candidate lists; repetition penalty = anti-loop; max tokens/stop = brakes.
  • Seeds make random sampling reproducible.
  • Settings polish style — competence comes from training (Parts VI–X).

Making Fontaine Smarter

Chapter 27

Fine-Tuning

A pretrained model knows how language flows. Fine-tuning teaches it what to do with that fluency — additional specialized training after the general stage.

Analogy — education stages

Pretraining is general schooling: years of reading everything, absorbing language, facts, and patterns — expensive and broad. Fine-tuning is professional training: a shorter, focused program on top of the education — medical school for doctors, a coding bootcamp for developers. Nobody confuses the two; the second is only possible because the first happened, and it would be absurd to re-raise the child for every new career.

What is it, technically?

Continue training a pretrained model — same architecture, same loss machinery — but on a smaller, targeted dataset, typically with a smaller learning rate and fewer steps. The weights start from the pretrained value instead of random noise, so the model is adapting existing understanding rather than learning language from zero.

Why is it different from training from scratch?

Scale of everything. Pretraining: billions of tokens, weeks of compute, learning language itself. Fine-tuning: thousands to millions of examples, hours to days, learning a behavior on top of language. A laptop can fine-tune small models; only data centers pretrain — one reason the two are never casually swapped.

Instruction tuning vs. domain fine-tuning

Instruction tuning (next chapter) teaches the model to respond to requests using prompt→response pairs. Domain fine-tuning adapts style and knowledge to a field — legal drafting, medical summaries, your company's documentation voice. Same mechanism, different datasets.

Honest Limitation

Fine-tuning is not a knowledge-upload button. It shapes behavior, format, and style far more reliably than it injects retrievable facts — and it can amplify biases present in the fine-tuning data. “Teach it our documents” often wants retrieval (a search system feeding the context window), not weight changes.

Why This Matters

Fontaine's design anticipates this stage precisely: the loss already supports masking (Ch 15), the pipeline already parses instruction-format records (Ch 5), and the checkpoint format already records lineage — so a fine-tuned model traces back to its pretrained parent. Phase 7 of the roadmap (Ch 41) is exactly this.

Key Takeaways
  • Pretraining = broad language; fine-tuning = targeted behavior on top of it.
  • Fine-tuning starts from learned weights — cheaper, shorter, behavior-focused.
  • Instruction tuning and domain tuning share one mechanism with different data.
  • Fine-tuning shapes style more than it uploads facts — retrieval often fits facts better.
Chapter 28

Instruction Following

A freshly pretrained model is a brilliant autocomplete — ask it a question and it may continue with more questions. Instruction tuning is what turns autocomplete into an assistant.

The problem: a pretrained model answers with, not to

Trained purely to continue text, a model asked “What is the capital of France?” may reply “What is the capital of Germany?” — because on the internet, questions are often followed by other questions! It is doing its job perfectly; its job just isn't yours yet. Instruction following is the skill of treating the user's text as a request to fulfill rather than a passage to continue.

How it's taught

The magic is generalization from format: from enough varied pairs, the model learns the role — “when text looks like a request, behave like a responder” — and applies it to requests it has never seen, in formats you never listed. That is the same pattern-generalization it learned in pretraining, now pointed at behavior instead of language flow.

Beginner Note — where do the example pairs come from?

Human-written demonstrations (expensive, high quality), carefully filtered model-generated examples (cheap, needs quality control), and your own domain Q&A. Quality bar matters more than quantity here — a few thousand excellent pairs beat a million sloppy ones, because the model learns your standards along with your tasks.

Key Takeaways
  • Pretrained models continue text; instruction tuning teaches them to fulfill requests.
  • Prompt→response pairs + response-only loss = the mechanism.
  • Generalization to unseen instructions is the payoff — a learned role, not memorized answers.
  • Pair quality dominates pair quantity.
Chapter 29

Preference Optimization

Instruction tuning teaches a model to answer. Preference optimization teaches it to answer well — according to human judgments about which of two answers is better. Conceptual tour only; this is frontier-lab territory that Fontaine's design leaves room for.

The core idea: teach from comparisons

Instead of “here is the right answer,” preference data says “here are two answers to the same prompt — humans judged this one better.” Comparisons are easier for humans to give than demonstrations (judging is easier than authoring), and they encode subtle qualities — helpfulness, tone, honesty — that are hard to write as rules. From many such judgments the model learns the direction of better.

The model learns the preferences of whoever made the comparisons — their biases, blind spots, and incentives included. Alignment to your values requires your judgment data at some stage, and no preference pipeline makes a model truthful in general; it makes it more like what was preferred.

Why This Matters

Fontaine's roadmap (Ch 41) sequences this deliberately: instruction tuning first (highest value per effort), then DPO-style preference work once real preference data exists, reward models only when there is something to model. Key Takeaways
  • Preference optimization learns “better vs. worse” from human comparisons rather than demonstrations.
  • DPO = offline preference learning; reward models + RL = the heavyweight frontier path.
  • The model absorbs its judges' values — choose your data accordingly.
  • Sequence it after instruction tuning; design first, implement much later.

Chapter 30

From Tiny to Large

The Fontaine ladder — five rungs, each a different configuration of the same architecture, each demanding a different tier of infrastructure.

What actually changes between rungs

Only four dials — plus one bill:

  • Width (hidden size) and depth (layers) — more capacity to hold finer patterns.
  • Context length — a wider field of view (RoPE's theta stretches with it, Ch 13).
  • Vocabulary — richer tokens, often multilingual.
  • Data — roughly 20 training tokens per parameter is the classic rule of thumb: a 28 M model wants ~600 M tokens; a 1 B model, ~20 B; a 70 B model, ~1.4 T.
  • Compute — the multiplication of all of the above, paid in GPU-hours.
Does more parameters automatically mean better?

No — and this is one of the field's most expensive misconceptions. Bigger models learn finer patterns only if fed enough quality data; an underfed large model loses to a well-fed small one. Quality of data, training discipline, and evaluation all gate what size can express. Size is a ceiling-raiser, not a floor-guarantor.

Why This Matters

Because Fontaine's promise is that rung-hopping is a configuration change plus an infrastructure change — never a rewrite. The interfaces you met (trainer, checkpoints, evaluators, config) were built exactly so this figure stays honest.

Key Takeaways
  • Five rungs, one architecture; sizes live in configs, infrastructure differs.
  • Width, depth, context, vocabulary, data — and compute as their product.
  • ~20 tokens/param is the classic data sizing rule.
  • Size raises the ceiling of possible quality; it guarantees nothing.
Chapter 31

Why Bigger Models Need More Hardware

Four walls, each hit in a different way: memory, compute, data, and coordination. This chapter is the arithmetic behind “why can't I just make it bigger?”

Wall 1 — Memory: the 16-bytes rule grows teeth

Chapter 17's rule — 16 bytes per parameter before activations — scales linearly but arrives brutally: 1 B parameters ≈ 16 GB just for training state, before the first activation. Activations add more, growing with batch × context × layers. Inference escapes some of this (no gradients or optimizer states — roughly 2 bytes/param when quantized) which is why a 16 GB machine can serve a 1–2 B model yet never train one. Memory is the first wall every scaler hits.

Wall 2 — Compute: the years-and-electricity problem

Training compute scales with parameters × data tokens — multiply Chapter 30's ladder by its data rule and watch: a 1 B model at 20 B tokens is ~1023 arithmetic operations. On one consumer GPU that is years of wall-clock time. Frontier runs buy their way out with thousands of GPUs working simultaneously — which converts a time problem into a money-and-coordination problem, not a solved one.

Wall 3 — Data: the quiet requirement

Every parameter increase raises the data bill by ~20 tokens per new parameter (Ch 30). Getting quality data at trillion-token scale means industrial collection, cleaning (Ch 6 at industrial strength), licensing clarity, and storage measured in terabytes of processed text. Data pipelines that were a directory of files become distributed systems.

Wall 4 — Coordination: the hidden cost of many hands

Once the work spans many GPUs, time is spent talking about the work: synchronizing gradients (Ch 32), balancing pipeline stages, tolerating machine failures mid-run (Ch 33). Coordination overhead is why scaling efficiency falls as clusters grow — 10 GPUs never do 10× the useful work of one, and the gap widens with size. Networking and storage cease to be afterthoughts and become design constraints.

Key Takeaways
  • Memory: 16 B/param training state + activations — linear in size, brutal in effect.
  • Compute: params × tokens → years on one GPU; clusters buy time with money and complexity.
  • Data: +20 quality tokens per new parameter, at industrial pipeline strength.
  • Coordination overhead grows with cluster size — scaling efficiency is never 100%.
Chapter 32

Multiple GPUs

One GPU stops being enough. The fix is division of labor — and there are three classic ways to divide it, each with a different thing being split.

Data parallelism — clone the teacher

The simplest split: give every GPU a full copy of the model, feed each a different slice of the batch, and let each compute its own gradients. Then the GPUs briefly compare notes — averaging their gradients so every clone applies the same combined update — and continue. The model fits on one GPU; the data is what's parallel. This is the first rung of multi-GPU training and the easiest to reason about: one teacher's lesson plan, many classrooms, one synchronized staff meeting per step.

Tensor parallelism — split the matrix

When the model itself doesn't fit on one GPU, split inside the layers: the giant weight matrices of attention and FFNs (Ch 14) are sliced across GPUs, each computing a piece of every operation. Fast — but pieces must converse constantly, requiring very high-bandwidth connections, so tensor parallelism lives within one machine.

Pipeline parallelism — split the depth

Instead split by layers: GPU 1 holds blocks 1–4, GPU 2 blocks 5–8, and so on — an assembly line where each stage processes its part and passes the work along. Communication is lighter (only stage boundaries talk), but the line has idle bubbles unless several batches flow at once, like a bucket brigade that only works while buckets keep arriving.

Technical Note — sharding the optimizer too (FSDP / ZeRO)

Between data parallelism and tensor parallelism sits a pragmatic middle: keep full model copies but shard the training state — parameters, gradients, optimizer memory — across GPUs, all-to-all gathering each piece just when needed (FSDP / DeepSpeed ZeRO). It extends data-parallel simplicity to models that no longer fit one GPU, and it is exactly where Fontaine's strategy seam points after DDP.

Key Takeaways
  • Data parallelism splits the batch; tensor parallelism the matrices; pipeline parallelism the layers.
  • Each strategy trades communication cost against memory relief differently.
  • FSDP/ZeRO sharded training state bridges the gap for mid-size scaling.
  • Frontier runs combine all three — 3D parallelism.

Chapter 36

Configuration

Every number that defines “which model, trained how, on what data” lives in configuration files — never buried in code. This is why Fontaine sizes are files, not forks.

Why configuration files exist — and why hard-coding is bad

If “make the model bigger” meant editing source code, every experiment would risk breaking the program, sizes couldn't be shared or compared, and reproducing an old run would mean archaeology. Hard-coded values also hide: a buried constant can contradict another buried constant and nothing complains. A configuration file is the opposite — declarative, diffable, shareable, and checked as a whole: Fontaine validates that sizes fit together (heads divide evenly, context lengths agree) before a run starts.

Chapter 37

Data Pipeline Architecture

Chapter 6 explained why each cleaning step exists. This chapter shows the machine that executes them — and its defining trick: the data is never fully in memory.

The three memory tricks, revisited as architecture. (1) Streaming readers yield one document at a time. (2) The shard writer holds exactly one fixed-size buffer and flushes it to disk when full — shard size, not corpus size, sets memory use. (3) Training reads by memory-mapping: the dataset opens shard files and pages in only the windows a batch needs, so training touches the corpus through a keyhole, on demand.

The manifest is the contract. When preparation finishes, it writes manifest.json: dataset name and version, source, license, language, document and token counts, per-shard checksums, tokenizer version, pipeline version, creation date. Training refuses to start without it; validation re-computes checksums to prove the shards on disk are the shards described. This is Chapter 6's provenance promise, implemented as a load-bearing file.

Technical Note — the train/val split lives here

The pipeline assigns whole documents to train or validation with a seeded random draw (val_fraction), writing them into separate shard sets. Document-level splitting prevents train/val leakage — near-identical documents landing on both sides — which would make validation loss lie flatteringly.

Key Takeaways
  • Stages are streaming iterators; memory is bounded by shard size, never corpus size.
  • Training reads through memory-mapping — keyhole access on demand.
  • The manifest is a checksummed contract: identity, provenance, and integrity in one file.
  • The train/val split happens at document level, seeded, before training ever begins.
Chapter 38

Training Architecture

Part VI explained the training step; this chapter introduces the cast that performs it — and the rule that keeps them cleanly separated.

The contracts, in one breath. The DataLoader promises batches shaped {input_ids, labels}. The model promises “tokens in → logits and loss out.” The optimizer and scheduler own every parameter change. The CheckpointManager owns persistence (and is told what to save, never how). Evaluators answer a single method call with metric numbers. The experiment record captures config, metrics, and logs. The trainer orchestrates — and knows nothing about Transformers, tokenizers, or file formats beyond those promises.

  • The trainer's ignorance of specifics is a feature: it's what survives scaling.
Chapter 39

Evaluation Architecture

Chapter 23 listed what to measure. Here is how new measurements are added without rewriting the model or the trainer — the registry pattern, in plain language.

A registry is a phone book for capabilities: each evaluator registers under a name (“validation_loss”, someday “instruction_following”), and configuration refers to evaluators by name. The trainer asks the phone book, not its own code. Consequence: a new benchmark is a new module plus a config line — the training engine is untouched, because it never knew the list of benchmarks to begin with.

# evaluation section of a config — benchmarks as data, not code
evaluation:
  evaluators:
    - name: validation_loss        # built in (Ch 23)
      params: { max_batches: 64 }
    # someday, registered the same way:
    # - name: instruction_following
    # - name: domain_qa

What it does: selects measurements by name. Why it exists: benchmarks multiply over a model's life; hard-coding them would mean editing the trainer for every new test. The important line: the name — the indirection is the entire architecture.

Every evaluator receives the same toolkit — the model, the evaluation data loader, the tokenizer, the generation engine — and returns metric numbers. Generative benchmarks of the future (question answering, summaries, code) will run through the standard inference engine, so they automatically inherit every inference improvement. One harness, growing forever.

Key Takeaways
  • Evaluators register by name; configs select them — the trainer stays generic.
  • New benchmark = new module + one config line. Nothing else changes.
  • Future generative benchmarks reuse the inference engine itself.
Chapter 40

Inference Architecture

Chapter 25 walked one prompt through one model. This chapter is the road from that single-user moment to a production model server serving many at once.

What a production layer adds, and why each piece exists: a gateway (one public door, TLS encryption, rate limits), authentication (who may ask), a queue (requests wait politely instead of overwhelming the GPUs), a scheduler with batching (many waiting requests get processed together through the GPU — the difference between serving 10 and 1,000 users per card), and worker processes (each loads the model and pulls from the queue; crash one, the rest continue).

Beginner Note — what “streaming” means for users

Answers appear word-by-word not because the model types fast, but because the server streams each generated token to your screen as it is produced (Ch 25's decode loop) — the same mechanism Fontaine's dev server and every commercial chat UI use. Latency you feel is mostly streaming design, not model speed.

Fontaine's staging: the local engine already separates concerns cleanly — checkpoint loading, the KV cache, sampling, and streaming each live behind their own small piece. The production ladder adds infrastructure around the Generator, never inside it. Even quantization (smaller number formats for serving, Ch 31) is a load-time change behind the same interface.

Key Takeaways
  • Local engine → gateway+queue+batching → GPU worker cluster: three rungs, one Generator interface.
  • Gateway/auth/queue exist to protect GPUs and users from chaos, politely.
  • Batching is the economic heart of serving — one GPU, many users.
  • Streaming is why answers feel instant; it rides the decode loop you already know.

Common Misunderstandings

Eleven Misunderstandings, Corrected

Fontaine computes geometric relationships between token vectors (Ch 9, 12). The behavior resembles understanding — appropriate continuations, coherent reasoning-shaped text — but the mechanism shares nothing with human comprehension: no intentions, no world-experience, no inner life. Behaviorally useful, mechanistically alien. Both facts matter when deciding what to trust a model with.

Size raises the ceiling of what can be learned (Ch 30) — and nothing else. An underfed billion-parameter model loses to a well-fed small one: data quality (Ch 5), training discipline (Part VI), and evaluation honesty (Part VII) gate everything. Size is one bill you pay, not a result you receive.

Chapter 6's gauntlet exists because raw volume teaches noise as readily as signal: duplicates cause parroting, spam teaches spam, junk encoding teaches junk. A million clean tokens out-educate a billion polluted ones. Quality > quantity > diversity — in that order.

Training changes parameters — gradients, optimizer states, hours-to-months (Ch 18–21). Inference freezes them — forward passes and a KV cache, seconds (Ch 25). Different memory profiles, different hardware economics, different failure modes. A model is built once and used forever; conflating the two makes both seem impossibly expensive.

A CPU is a few enormously capable cores for sequential logic; a GPU is thousands of modest cores for simultaneous identical arithmetic (Ch 16). The Transformer is precisely that second kind of work — millions of independent multiply-adds — which is why GPUs accelerate it 100× while a CPU isn't “slow,” merely built for a different job. Also remember VRAM ≠ RAM: the GPU computes in its own small memory (Ch 17).

It cannot — by arithmetic, not opinion: 16 bytes/parameter of training state (Ch 17) excludes billion-scale models on memory alone, and compute (Ch 31) excludes them again by years. A 16 GB machine is a genuine development environment — Fontaine Tiny through Medium, full pipeline, full craft — and structurally incapable of frontier pretraining. This book's honesty rule is built on that sentence.

The tokenizer is frozen bookkeeping — a two-way text↔number converter with no learning and no understanding (Ch 8). It shapes what the model can easily learn (granularity, language costs) but contains none of the intelligence. Swap it and the model's brain is untouched; retrain the model and the tokenizer never changes.

No document is stored anywhere you could point to (Ch 10). Knowledge is distributed across parameters as learned patterns — which is why models can produce novel sentences, why they sometimes generalize brilliantly, and why heavy duplication (Ch 6) can still cause parroting of over-represented text. “Pattern, not filing cabinet” is the correct default picture.

Same loss machinery, opposite scales and purposes (Ch 27): pretraining learns language itself from billions of tokens on data-center hardware; fine-tuning adapts behavior from thousands-to-millions of examples on hardware you may own. Starting weights differ too — pretrained vs. random — which is the entire point.

A bigger window means the model may see more — not that it reasons better over what it sees (Ch 13). Attention must learn to use distant information; utilization varies wildly by model and training. Also, cost grows quadratically with context (Ch 12, 31). A window is a desk size; reasoning is how well you work at it.

Benchmarks are narrow instruments with real failure modes — training contamination, style overfitting, popular-set leakage (Ch 23). A score is one opinion as a number; it cannot see tone, honesty in your domain, or failure modes your tasks care about. Always triangulate: your own tasks, your own eyes, then benchmarks as supporting evidence.

Why This Matters

Each correction above is a chapter you have already read, wearing a myth's clothing. If you can correct all eleven from memory, the book has done its job — you are reasoning about Fontaine, not reciting it.

Complete End-to-End Story

One hypothetical, followed unbroken from a folder of documents to a user's answer — every component of the book appearing exactly once, in order, in the role you now understand.

From Raw Text to Fontaine AI

The cast: a folder with 10,000 documents, a 16 GB machine, Fontaine Tiny, and one patient user. Everything between is the book, retold as one afternoon.

Act I — the data becomes training-ready

1
The folder arrives

10,000 documents: notes, articles, dialogues. What it is: potential. Nothing yet knows it exists. (Ch 5 — the library that defines what can be learned.)

2
The pipeline cleans

Broken encodings fixed, 412 too-short fragments dropped, 931 duplicates removed (one kept per repeat), unicode normalized. 8,400 documents remain. Why: each removal prevents a specific future failure — parroting, garbage tokens, split meanings. (Ch 6.)

3
Validation seals it

Counts, statistics, and per-shard checksums are written into a manifest; 5% of documents are set aside as the never-trained validation slice. Why: Chapter 19's honest exam must exist, and integrity must be provable. (Ch 6, 19, 37.)

4
The tokenizer is built and applied

From a sample of the corpus, byte-level BPE finds ~4,000 reusable fragments — words, subwords, bytes as the safety net — each with a token ID. All 8,400 documents become one long stream of numbers, joined by end-of-document markers, cut into windows, written to binary shards. RAM barely notices; the corpus was streamed. (Ch 7, 8, 37.)

Act II — the model learns

5
Fontaine Tiny wakes up, random

4 blocks, hidden width 256, ~4 million parameters — every one a random number. Its first predictions are indistinguishable from noise, and that is normal. (Ch 15, 20.)

6
The loop spins

Each step: a batch of token windows flows through embeddings, RoPE, and the block stack (forward pass); the model guesses every position's next token; loss measures the surprise; backpropagation gives all 4 million parameters their “which way” notes; AdamW applies the nudges. Steps 1–50 climb steeply — loss falls from “uniform guessing.” (Ch 18, 19, 20.)

7
Learning becomes visible

By step 500 the model completes common phrases; by 2,000 it holds a sentence's style across its length; validation loss falls alongside training loss — the healthy pair from Figure 19.1, patterns genuinely transferring. (Ch 19, 23.)

8
Checkpoints accumulate

Every 250 steps a save-game lands on disk: weights, optimizer memory, schedule, random seeds, provenance, checksums. At step 3,750 the validation curve bottoms out — the best pointer moves. Training pauses; nothing is lost. (Ch 22, 24.)

9
Evaluation judges

Perplexity lands around 3–4 on held-out text: effectively “choosing among a handful of candidates” per token — remarkable for 4 M parameters, modest by design. Sampling a dozen generations by hand completes the verdict: coherent, narrow, honest about its size. (Ch 23.)

Act III — the model speaks

10
A user asks

“What does the fountain symbolize in the stories?” The engine loads the best checkpoint (architecture read from the checkpoint's own metadata), tokenizes the question — maybe 12 tokens — and runs one prefill pass, filling the KV cache. (Ch 22, 25.)

11
Tokens, one by one

The decode loop: probabilities for the whole vocabulary, temperature 0.8 shaping the choice, top-p trimming the tail, the chosen token appended, attention reading it through the cache. Dozens of beats — “In” → “these” → “stories” → … — until <|eos|> closes the answer. (Ch 26.)

12
The answer decodes

The token stream becomes text. It is small-model prose: correct within its corpus's world, simple beyond it. The user received, in one second of inference, the compressed result of an afternoon of preparation and training — and months of understanding that made it possible. (Ch 25.)

A standard piece of text — a word, subword fragment, character, or byte — the smallest unit the model reads (Ch 7).
Tokenizer
The frozen converter between text and token numbers (encoding and decoding); owns the vocabulary (Ch 8).
Vocabulary
The master list of all tokens the tokenizer and model share, each with a fixed ID (Ch 8).
Embedding
The learned vector (list of numbers) representing a token's meaning-profile; stored as a row of the embedding table (Ch 9).
Parameter
Any adjustable number inside the model — weights, biases, embedding values. “4M parameters” = 4 million learned numbers (Ch 10).
Weight
A parameter acting as an importance score on a connection between units (Ch 10).
Bias
A small offset parameter added before a neuron's squashing step, letting it shift its threshold (Ch 10, 20).
Attention
The mechanism letting each position gather information from other positions with learned strength (Ch 12).
Self-Attention
Attention where a sequence relates to itself — every position queries every other (Ch 12).
Head
One parallel instance of attention with its own learned projections; multiple heads track different relationship types (Ch 12).
GQA (Grouped-Query Attention)
Sharing Keys/Values among groups of heads to shrink the KV cache with minimal quality cost (Ch 12, 25).
Layer
One stage of processing; the Transformer's blocks are stacked layers (Ch 10, 14).
Activation
(1) A neuron's squashed output value; (2) the stored intermediate results of a forward pass — a major memory consumer (Ch 10, 17).
Loss
One number measuring how wrong the model's predictions were; cross-entropy for language models (Ch 19).
Cross-Entropy
The loss function: negative log of the probability the model assigned to the true next token (Ch 19).
Gradient
Per-parameter information about which direction of change would reduce the error; computed by backpropagation (Ch 18).
Backpropagation
The algorithm that computes all gradients by running the network's calculations backwards from the loss (Ch 18).
Optimizer
The algorithm converting gradients into parameter updates — how far and how cleverly to move (Ch 20).
Learning Rate
The step size of each parameter update; too big diverges, too small crawls (Ch 20).
AdamW
The optimizer Fontaine uses: per-parameter step-size memory plus weight decay (Ch 20).
Weight Decay
A gentle pull of parameters toward zero each step, favoring simpler patterns (Ch 20).
Batch
The set of examples processed together in one training step (Ch 21).
Gradient Accumulation
Summing gradients over several small micro-batches to simulate one large batch without the memory (Ch 21).
Epoch
One complete pass over the whole training dataset (Ch 21).
Checkpoint
A saved training state — weights, optimizer, schedule, randomness, metadata — enabling resume and deployment (Ch 22).
Inference
Using the frozen trained model to generate output; the forward pass without any learning (Ch 25).
KV Cache
Stored Keys and Values of past positions during generation, so each new token is cheap to compute (Ch 25).
Fine-Tuning
Additional targeted training of a pretrained model on a smaller, specific dataset (Ch 27).
Pretraining
The initial broad training that teaches language itself, from random weights, on massive data (Ch 27).
Instruction Tuning
Fine-tuning on prompt→response pairs so the model answers requests instead of merely continuing text (Ch 28).
DPO (Direct Preference Optimization)
A method that fine-tunes a model directly on human preference comparisons (chosen vs. rejected answers) (Ch 29).
Reward Model
A separate model trained to score outputs like humans would, used to guide RL-based training (Ch 29).
GPU
Graphics Processing Unit — thousands of arithmetic cores working simultaneously; the natural engine of Transformer math (Ch 16).
VRAM
The GPU's own working memory, separate from and usually smaller than system RAM; where training and inference live (Ch 16, 17).
RAM
The computer's temporary working memory; 16 GB is the ceiling of this book's reference machine (Ch 16, 17).
Mixed Precision
Doing arithmetic in half-size floating-point numbers (bf16/fp16) to save memory and gain speed (Ch 17).
Gradient Checkpointing
Recomputing activations when needed instead of storing them — memory saved by paying compute (Ch 17).
Distributed Training
Training across many GPUs or machines working in coordination (Ch 32, 33).
Data Parallelism
Cloning the model on each GPU and splitting the batch among them (Ch 32).
Tensor Parallelism
Splitting individual layers' matrices across GPUs within a machine (Ch 32).
Pipeline Parallelism
Splitting the layer stack into stages across GPUs, like an assembly line (Ch 32).
FSDP / ZeRO Sharding
Splitting parameters, gradients, and optimizer states across GPUs, gathering pieces just in time (Ch 32).
Quantization
Representing model weights (or activations) in smaller number formats for cheaper inference (Ch 31).
Context Window
The maximum number of tokens the model can attend to in one pass (Ch 13, 31).
RoPE (Rotary Position Embedding)
Encoding token positions as vector rotations so attention naturally sees relative distance (Ch 13).
Perplexity
Loss re-expressed as “effectively choosing among N equally likely options”; lower is better (Ch 23).
Overfitting
Memorizing training data instead of learning patterns; validation loss rises while training loss falls (Ch 24).
Underfitting
Failing to learn even the training patterns — both losses stay high (Ch 24).
Validation
Measuring on held-out data the model never trained on — the honest exam (Ch 19, 23).
Dataset Manifest
The checksummed metadata contract describing a prepared dataset: identity, provenance, counts, integrity (Ch 6, 37).
Data Provenance
The recorded origin of each piece of training data — source, license, processing history (Ch 6).
Model Serving
Running a model as a service for many users — gateways, queues, batching, GPU workers (Ch 40).
Streaming (generation)
Sending each generated token to the user as it is produced, so answers appear word-by-word (Ch 25, 26, 40).

Reading the diagram, simply

The two pillars. Everything Fontaine is hangs on Data and Model — because a language model is exactly “patterns learned from data, embodied in a network” (Ch 3, 5). Neither pillar is optional, and neither substitutes for the other: a perfect model with no data learns nothing; a perfect dataset without a network cannot predict.

The left pillar: data becomes training fuel. Preparation cleans, filters, deduplicates, and validates your raw text (Ch 6). Tokenization converts it to numbers the machine can read (Part III). Training here means the packed, streamed training material ready for the loop (Ch 37). Checkpoints on the data side are the manifests — the checksummed contracts proving what the data is and where it came from (Ch 6, 37).

The right pillar: the model becomes useful. The Transformer is the architecture you assembled in Part IV. Training is the learning loop of Part VI. Evaluation is Part VII's honesty layer — the exam that training loss alone cannot give. Inference is Part VIII — the frozen model answering, one token at a time. The model-side checkpoints are Chapter 22's save-games, the thread connecting training to evaluation to deployment.

The platform beneath. Both pillars rest on the same platform: the configuration system, the pipelines, the checkpoint formats, the registries — Part XI's software architecture. That platform is what makes the three futures at the bottom of the diagram possible:

  • Local → small models. Today, on one 16 GB machine: Fontaine Tiny through Medium, the whole craft on your desk (Ch 17).
  • Cloud → larger models. Rented GPUs, the same code with bigger configs and the memory techniques of Chapter 17 stretched further.
  • Distributed → massive models. The fleet of Chapters 32–33: parallelism strategies, sharded everything, engineered reliability — the honest, expensive path toward the systems of Chapter 34.
Why This Matters — the promise, restated precisely

The diagram's deepest claim is the shape: the same platform, the same concepts, the same interfaces span all three futures. What grows is infrastructure, data, and team. What transfers — intact, from your laptop to any future cluster — is the architecture and your understanding of it.

The last honest word

A 16 GB machine will never train a GPT or Gemini. It will do something arguably more valuable to you: make you the person who genuinely understands how such systems are built — because you built the whole thing once, small and honest, with your own hands. Every large system in the world is this book's diagram, with bigger numbers and more engineers. You now read that diagram fluently.
